\section{Introduction}

Volcanic eruptions are among the most destructive natural hazards, posing serious risks to human life, infrastructure, aviation, and the environment. Their impacts extend beyond the eruptive event itself, producing both direct and indirect hazards. Direct hazards include pyroclastic density currents, lava flows, tephra fall, and ballistic projectiles, whereas indirect hazards such as lahars, landslides, and sediment remobilisation may continue long after eruptive activity has ceased \cite{Tilling1989}. Together, these hazards have caused substantial loss of life and widespread damage to infrastructure throughout history.

Globally, approximately 800 million people are estimated to live within 100 km of an active volcano, increasing exposure to volcanic hazards \cite{Brown2015}. Fertile volcanic soils encourage agriculture and human settlement, while spectacular volcanic landscapes attract increasing numbers of tourists. Consequently, both permanent residents and visitors may be exposed to volcanic hazards during periods of unrest \cite{Small2001,Auker2013,Erfurt2022}. In addition to local impacts, explosive eruptions may inject volcanic ash into the atmosphere, posing serious risks to aviation and causing significant economic losses through disruption of international air traffic \cite{Prata2009,Tilling1989}.

Whakaari (White Island), located offshore of New Zealand's North Island, is one of the country's most active volcanoes and is characterised by persistent hydrothermal activity. On 9 December 2019, Whakaari erupted unexpectedly while tourists were visiting the island, resulting in 22 fatalities and numerous serious injuries. The eruption highlighted the challenges associated with forecasting phreatic eruptions, which often occur with limited or ambiguous precursory signals. Although volcanic monitoring systems indicated ongoing unrest before the eruption, accurately identifying the transition from unrest to eruption remains a significant scientific challenge. This event has motivated continued research into objective, data-driven approaches capable of extracting subtle changes from continuous volcanic monitoring data.

\subsection{Seismic monitoring and RSAM}

Reducing volcanic risk relies on continuous monitoring capable of detecting changes within the volcanic system before hazardous activity occurs. In New Zealand, active volcanoes are monitored through the GeoNet programme using a multidisciplinary approach that combines seismic and acoustic observations, ground deformation measurements, volcanic gas monitoring, thermal measurements, visual observations, and geochemical analyses. These complementary observations provide insight into subsurface magma migration, hydrothermal activity, and the evolving state of volcanic unrest, supporting the assessment of volcanic hazards and the communication of volcanic alert levels to emergency management agencies and the public \cite{GNSVolcanoMonitoring}. Although multiple monitoring techniques contribute to understanding volcanic behavior, seismic observations remain one of the most continuous and widely available sources of information because they directly record changes associated with magma movement, rock fracturing, and hydrothermal processes. Consequently, seismic data have become fundamental for investigating the temporal evolution of volcanic systems and developing quantitative methods for analysing volcanic unrest.

One of the most widely used quantitative measures of volcanic seismic activity is the Real-time Seismic Amplitude Measurement (RSAM), introduced by Endo and Murray~\cite{Endo1991}. RSAM represents the average seismic amplitude computed over a predefined time interval and frequency band, providing a continuous measure of volcanic tremor intensity. Owing to its computational efficiency and operational simplicity, RSAM has become a standard monitoring parameter at volcano observatories worldwide and has been widely used to identify changes in volcanic activity and support eruption monitoring \cite{Endo1991}. Nevertheless, most existing studies rely primarily on amplitude trends, spectral characteristics, or statistical summaries of the seismic signal.

Volcanic tremor is widely regarded as one of the most informative seismic signals for monitoring active volcanoes because it reflects fluid movement, magma transport, and hydrothermal processes occurring beneath the volcanic edifice. Consequently, RSAM and related seismic amplitude measures have been extensively used for monitoring volcanic unrest and have shown considerable potential for short-term eruption forecasting. Nevertheless, many existing approaches focus primarily on changes in signal amplitude or frequency characteristics, leaving the temporal organisation and dynamical complexity of the seismic signal comparatively unexplored.

\subsection{Information-theoretic analysis of seismic signals}

Information-theoretic approaches have increasingly been applied to volcanic seismic monitoring because they provide insight into the underlying dynamics of seismic signals beyond conventional amplitude-based measures. Unlike traditional parameters such as the Real-time Seismic Amplitude Measurement (RSAM), which primarily quantify signal intensity, information-theoretic measures characterise the temporal structure, uncertainty, and complexity of seismic signals. Consequently, they have shown considerable potential for identifying changes associated with magma migration, volcanic unrest, and eruptive activity.

Telesca et al.~\cite{Telesca2014} were among the first to demonstrate the usefulness of information-theoretic measures for analysing continuous volcanic seismic signals. They applied the Fisher--Shannon method to seismic data recorded during the 2011--2012 El Hierro eruption in the Canary Islands. Their analysis showed that the Fisher Information Measure (FIM) was particularly sensitive to transitions between different stages of volcanic activity, including magma intrusion, hydraulic fracturing, magma migration, conduit opening, and eruptive tremor. These changes were not always evident from conventional seismic amplitude measurements alone, demonstrating that information-theoretic measures can reveal changes in the physical state of the volcanic system and dynamics that complement traditional monitoring techniques.

Building upon this work, Rey-Devesa et al.~\cite{ReyDevesa2023} investigated whether Shannon entropy could be used to monitor explosive activity at Volc\'an de Colima, Mexico. Analysing continuous seismic records collected between 2015 and 2017, the authors observed a systematic decrease in Shannon entropy prior to major explosive eruptions. The entropy reduction occurred several days before eruption and reflected increasing regularity within the seismic signal as magma ascent became the dominant physical process. Their results demonstrated that Shannon entropy can provide useful precursor information and may complement conventional monitoring parameters by detecting subtle dynamical changes before explosive events.

The applicability of Shannon entropy was subsequently extended by Rey-Devesa et al.~\cite{ReyDevesa2023JGR}, who evaluated the method across five volcanoes with contrasting eruptive styles, including Mount Etna, Mount St.~Helens, Kīlauea, Augustine Volcano, and Bezymianny. Despite the differences in volcanic behaviour, all case studies exhibited a consistent decline in Shannon entropy prior to eruption, with precursor times ranging from several hours to approximately four days. The study demonstrated that Shannon entropy provides a computationally efficient and transferable early-warning indicator that can operate directly on continuous seismic data without requiring volcano-specific physical models or extensive training datasets.

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that information-theoretic measures provide valuable complementary information to conventional seismic monitoring by quantifying the evolving temporal structure and complexity of volcanic seismic signals. However, existing studies primarily estimate Shannon entropy and Fisher information directly from the statistical distribution of continuous seismic waveforms. In contrast, the present study employs an ordinal-pattern framework to construct probability distributions from the temporal ordering of seismic observations and subsequently computes Shannon, R\'enyi, and Tsallis entropies together with Fisher information and statistical complexity. By integrating multiple information-theoretic measures within the ordinal pattern framework, this study aims to provide a more comprehensive characterisation of the temporal dynamics and complexity of volcanic seismic activity.

Although these studies demonstrate that information-theoretic measures can provide valuable insights into volcanic seismic activity and show promise for eruption monitoring, accurately forecasting volcanic eruptions remains a major challenge. Volcano observatories routinely issue Volcanic Alert Levels (VALs) to communicate the current state of volcanic unrest; however, these classifications primarily describe the current condition of a volcano rather than providing quantitative forecasts of the timing or likelihood of future eruptions. Forecasting is particularly challenging for hydrothermal systems such as Whakaari, where rapid changes may occur with limited or ambiguous precursory activity. These challenges motivate the development of objective computational techniques capable of extracting additional information from continuous monitoring data. In this context, information-theoretic approaches based on ordinal patterns offer a promising framework for characterising subtle changes in the temporal dynamics of seismic signals that may not be evident using conventional monitoring parameters alone.

\subsection{Research Motivation and Objectives}

Despite these encouraging developments, relatively few studies have investigated volcanic seismic activity using ordinal-pattern-based information-theoretic analysis. Ordinal patterns transform a time series into symbolic sequences according to the relative ordering of neighbouring observations rather than their absolute amplitudes \cite{Bandt2002}. This representation is naturally robust to observational noise and monotonic transformations while preserving the intrinsic temporal dynamics of the underlying process. Information-theoretic quantifiers derived from ordinal patterns including Shannon entropy, R\'enyi entropy, Tsallis entropy, Fisher information, and statistical complexity have demonstrated considerable success in characterising nonlinear dynamical systems across numerous scientific disciplines. Nevertheless, their application to long-term RSAM observations from Whakaari remains limited.

Motivated by this research gap, this study investigates the temporal dynamics of continuous RSAM observations recorded at the WIZ seismic station on Whakaari (White Island), New Zealand(Figure~\ref{fig:whakaari}). Rather than developing an eruption forecasting model, the objective is to characterise volcanic seismic activity using ordinal pattern based information-theoretic descriptors and to investigate whether changes in entropy and complexity coincide with documented eruptive episodes. The proposed framework aims to provide additional insight into the temporal behavior of volcanic seismic signals and to evaluate the usefulness of ordinal pattern based feature extraction for analysing long-term volcanic monitoring data.

\section{Study Area and Data}

\subsection{Study area}
Whakaari (White Island), located approximately 48 km offshore from the eastern coast of New Zealand's North Island, is one of the country's most active stratovolcanoes. The volcano has exhibited persistent hydrothermal activity and numerous eruptive episodes over recent decades, making it an important natural laboratory for volcano monitoring research. Continuous seismic observations collected at the WIZ seismic station provide one of the longest and most comprehensive records of volcanic tremor available for Whakaari. Previous studies have demonstrated that RSAM measurements derived from these observations contain valuable information regarding changes in volcanic activity and have been successfully employed for eruption monitoring and forecasting \cite{Dempsey2020}.

\subsection{Data Description}

The dataset analysed in this study consists of continuous Real-time Seismic Amplitude Measurement (RSAM) observations recorded at the WIZ seismic station on Whakaari (White Island), New Zealand, from 2007 to 2022 (Figure~\ref{fig:ts_2007-2022}). The original seismic waveform was sampled at a frequency of \SI{100}{\hertz}. To reduce the data volume while preserving the temporal evolution of volcanic tremor, the waveform amplitudes were averaged over 10-minute intervals. Consequently, each 10-minute RSAM value represents the mean of 60,000 consecutive waveform measurements, providing a continuous record of volcanic seismic activity throughout the study period.

To illustrate the temporal characteristics of the data at different scales, Figures~\ref{fig:ts_2019}--\ref{fig:ts_9Dec2019} present progressively finer views of the 2019 time series. Figure~\ref{fig:ts_2019} shows the complete RSAM record for 2019, Figure~\ref{fig:ts_Dec2019} focuses on December 2019, and Figure~\ref{fig:ts_9Dec2019} presents the period from 8 to 10 December 2019, encompassing the Whakaari eruption on 9 December 2019. These visualisations demonstrate the short-term variability in volcanic tremor that motivates the subsequent ordinal pattern analysis.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
To study the temporal behavior of volcanic seismic activity, the ten-minutes RSAM time series was partitioned into non-overlapping one-week windows, each containing 1,008 observations. Each weekly segment was analysed independently using the ordinal pattern framework with embedding dimension $D=5$ and time delay $\tau=1$. For each window, the ordinal pattern probability distribution was constructed and used to compute a suite of information-theoretic quantifiers, including Shannon, R\'enyi, and Tsallis entropies, the Fisher Information Measure (FIM), and their corresponding statistical complexity measures. These quantifiers characterise complementary aspects of the seismic signal, such as uncertainty, temporal structure, and local regularity. Consequently, each one-week window is represented as a point in the corresponding $H \times C$ plane for the Shannon, R\'enyi, and Tsallis measures, and in the Fisher information complexity plane for the Fisher Information Measure. Tracking the trajectories of these points over time enables the temporal changes of the volcanic system to be investigated and facilitates the identification of changes associated with periods of volcanic unrest and eruptive activity.

Although the seismic waveform was averaged into 10-minute RSAM observations, the resulting time series still contains a large number of data points. To improve computational efficiency while maintaining adequate temporal resolution, the 2019 dataset was partitioned into overlapping weekly windows. Each window spans seven consecutive days, and successive windows begin six days after the previous one, resulting in a one-day overlap between adjacent windows. The original seismic waveform was first converted into a 10-minute average RSAM time series by averaging consecutive observations over non-overlapping 10-minute intervals. The resulting 10-minute RSAM series was then partitioned into overlapping weekly windows for analysis. Each window spans seven consecutive days, and successive windows begin six days after the previous window, producing a one-day overlap between adjacent weeks. The ordinal pattern distributions and the corresponding entropy and complexity measures were computed independently for each weekly window, allowing the temporal evolution of the volcanic system to be examined throughout 2019.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Information Theoretic Quantifiers}

For each ordinal pattern probability vector $\mathbf P=\{\mathbf p_\ell\}_{\ell=1}^{k}$, we evaluate a collection of 
global and local information measures.  
Given an estimator $\widehat{\mathbf P}=\{\widehat{\mathbf p}_\ell\}$ obtained from the empirical ordinal pattern frequencies, our goal is to study the behaviour of the entropy functionals $H(\widehat{ \mathbf P})$ and their associated complexity measures.

\paragraph{Permutation Entropy.}
Shannon entropy provides a quantitative measure of the uncertainty
encoded in the ordinal pattern distribution. Given the probability
vector
\(\widehat{\mathbf{p}} = (\widehat{p}_1,\widehat{p}_{2},\dots,\widehat{p}_k)\),
the normalized Shannon entropy, called Permutation entropy, is defined
as the Shannon entropy divided by its maximum value \(\log k\), i.e., \[
H(\widehat{\mathbf p})
=
-\frac{1}{\log k}
\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}
\widehat p_\ell \log \widehat p_\ell.
\] 
The normalization ensures that \(0\le H(\widehat{\mathbf p})\le1\), allowing comparisons across different embedding dimensions.

In the context of ordinal analysis, \(H(\widehat{\mathbf{p}})\) is commonly referred to as permutation entropy. It captures the degree of disorder in the symbolic dynamics: low values indicate strong regularity or determinism, while high values reflect randomness or
unpredictability. However, entropy alone cannot distinguish between randomness and structured complexity, motivating the introduction of complementary measures.

\paragraph{Tsallis entropy.}
For $q \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\}$, the Tsallis entropy is given by
\[
H^{\mathrm{T}}_{q}(\widehat{\mathbf P})
= \frac{1}{q-1}\left(1 - \sum_{\ell=1}^{k} \widehat{\mathbf p}_\ell^{\,q}\right),
\]
which reduces to Shannon entropy in the limit $q \to 1$.  
A normalized version is obtained by dividing by its value under the uniform distribution.

\paragraph{Rényi entropy.}
For $q>0$, $q\neq 1$, the Rényi entropy is defined as
\[
H^{\mathrm{R}}_{q}(\widehat{P})
= \frac{1}{1-q}\log\!\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{k} \widehat{\mathbf p}_\ell^{\,q}\right),
\]
and is likewise normalized using its maximum value at the uniform distribution.

\paragraph{Fisher Information Measure.}
To quantify local sensitivity in the ordinal pattern distribution we adopt the discrete Fisher Information Measure proposed by Rey et.al.~\cite{Rey2024Asymptotic}:
\[
F(\widehat{\mathbf P})
= 4\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}
(\sqrt{(\widehat{\mathbf p}_{\ell+1})}-\sqrt{(\widehat{\mathbf p}_{\ell})})^{2}.
\]
%\frac{(\widehat{\mathbf p}_{\ell+1}-\widehat{\mathbf p}_{\ell})^{2}}
%{\widehat{\mathbf p}_{\ell+1}+\widehat{\mathbf p}_{\ell}}.
%\]

This expression emphasizes differences between adjacent probabilities and therefore captures fine scale structure that entropy based measures may overlook.

\subsubsection{Disequilibrium}\label{disequilibrium}

All the entropies and FIM provide complementary information about the ordinal pattern distribution. However, neither descriptor quantifies the  degree to which the distribution deviates from the uniform distribution.
To incorporate this information, we consider the concept of disequilibrium introduced by~Lopez-Ruiz et al. (1995). In the ordinal pattern setting, the natural reference is the uniform distribution \(\mathbf{u}=(1/k,1/k,\ldots,1/k)\).

A widely adopted disequilibrium measure is based on the Jensen--Shannon divergence, which provides a symmetric, bounded, and information theoretically meaningful distance between \(\widehat{\mathbf{p}}\) and \(\mathbf{u}\): 
\[
J(\widehat{\mathbf{p}},\mathbf{u})
= H\!\left(\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{p}}+\mathbf{u}}{2}\right)
- \frac{1}{2}H(\widehat{\mathbf{p}})
- \frac{1}{2}\log(k).
\] 
The maximum possible divergence for a Jensen-Shannon divergence is 
\[
J_{\max}
= -2\left[\frac{(k+1)}{k}\log(k+1) - 2\log(2k) + \log k\right].
\] 
Using this, the normalized disequilibrium is defined as 
\[
Q(\widehat{\mathbf{p}}) = \frac{J(\widehat{\mathbf{p}},\mathbf{u})}{J_{\max}}.
\] 
This quantity satisfies that \(Q(\widehat{\mathbf{p}}) = 0\) for the uniform distribution (maximal disorder), and that \(Q(\widehat{\mathbf{p}}) = 1\) when all probability mass is concentrated on a single pattern (maximal deviation from equilibrium).

The same Jensen--Shannon-based disequilibrium is used throughout this study for constructing Shannon, Tsallis, Rényi, and Fisher complexity measures.

\subsubsection{Statistical Complexity Measures}
\label{complexity-measures}

Entropy and Fisher information quantify different aspects of the ordinal pattern distribution. Entropy measures describe the degree of uncertainty or disorder, whereas Fisher information captures local variations and sensitivity to changes in the distribution. However, these quantities
alone do not distinguish between complete randomness and structured organization. To account for both the amount of information and the departure from the uniform distribution, we consider statistical complexity measures.

Following the framework proposed Lamberti et al.~(2004)~\cite{lamberti2004intensive}, complexity is obtained as the product of an information measure and the normalized disequilibrium \(Q(\widehat{\mathbf p})\). This construction ensures that complexity is small both for highly ordered systems and for completely random systems, while attaining larger values for distributions exhibiting intermediate levels of organization.

The Shannon statistical complexity is defined as
\[
C_S(\widehat{\mathbf p})
=
H(\widehat{\mathbf p})
\,Q(\widehat{\mathbf p}),
\]
where \(H(\widehat{\mathbf p})\) is the normalized Shannon entropy.

Similarly, the Tsallis statistical complexity is
\[
C_T^{(q)}(\widehat{\mathbf p})
=
H_T^{(q)}(\widehat{\mathbf p})
\,Q(\widehat{\mathbf p}),
\]
where \(H_T^{(q)}(\widehat{\mathbf p})\) denotes the normalized Tsallis
entropy of order \(q\).

The Rényi statistical complexity is defined as
\[
C_R^{(q)}(\widehat{\mathbf p})
=
H_R^{(q)}(\widehat{\mathbf p})
\,Q(\widehat{\mathbf p}),
\]
where \(H_R^{(q)}(\widehat{\mathbf p})\) denotes the normalized Rényi
entropy of order \(q\).

Finally, a Fisher-based complexity measure is obtained by combining the Fisher Information Measure with the normalized disequilibrium:
\[
C_F(\widehat{\mathbf p})
=
F(\widehat{\mathbf p})
\,Q(\widehat{\mathbf p}).
\]

These complexity measures combine information about the uncertainty, local structure, and deviation from equilibrium of the ordinal pattern distribution, providing complementary descriptors for characterizing the underlying dynamics of the time series.


\subsection{Feature Extraction from Seismic Time Series}\label{feature_extraction}

Ordinal-pattern-based features are extracted from each time series according to the following procedure:

\begin{itemize}
	
	\item \textbf{Embedding:}
	A fixed embedding dimension ($D=5$) and time delay ($\tau=1$) are used to construct ordinal patterns from the seismic time series.
	
	\item \textbf{Temporal Aggregation:}
	The analysis considers monthly and hourly subsets of the RSAM time series for the period 2007-2022.
	
	\item \textbf{Feature Extraction:}
	For each time series, the ordinal pattern distribution is obtained and the following quantities are computed:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		
		\item \emph{Entropy Measures:}
		Shannon entropy, R\'enyi entropy, Tsallis entropy, and Fisher information measure.
		
		\item \emph{Complexity Measures:}
		The corresponding Shannon, R\'enyi, Tsallis, and Fisher complexity measures.
		
		\item \emph{Disequilibrium:}
		The Jensen--Shannon disequilibrium associated with the ordinal pattern distribution.
		
		\item \emph{Variance Measures:}
		Variance estimates for selected entropy and complexity measures to assess feature stability.
		
		\item \emph{Confidence Intervals:}
		Confidence intervals for entropy and complexity measures are computed to quantify estimation uncertainty.
		
	\end{itemize}
	
\end{itemize}

The analysis is based on the following thirteen ordinal-pattern features:

\begin{itemize}
	\item $H_{\text{Shannon}}$,
	$H_{\text{R\'enyi}}$,
	$H_{\text{Tsallis}}$,
	$H_{\text{Fisher}}$,
	
	\item $C_{\text{Shannon}}$,
	$C_{\text{R\'enyi}}$,
	$C_{\text{Tsallis}}$,
	$C_{\text{Fisher}}$,
	
	\item Disequilibrium,
	
	\item $\mathrm{Var}(H_{\text{Shannon}})$,
	$\mathrm{Var}(H_{\text{R\'enyi}})$,
	$\mathrm{Var}(H_{\text{Tsallis}})$,
	
	\item $\mathrm{Var}(C_{\text{Shannon}})$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Feature Space Representation}

This section presents the feature representations in the $H \times C$ plane derived from ordinal pattern analysis of the RSAM time series. Four information-theoretic feature spaces are considered: Shannon entropy and statistical complexity, Tsallis entropy and statistical complexity, R\'enyi entropy and statistical complexity, and Fisher information measure and its associated statistical complexity. These representations provide a compact visualisation of the temporal characteristics of the seismic amplitude data and allow comparisons between different observation periods.

Figure~\ref{fig:dec_HC_comparison} shows the annual feature representations obtained from the RSAM time series, where each point corresponds to one year between 2007 and 2021. The four panels compare the distributions of the annual feature vectors in the different $H \times C$ planes. Although inter-annual variability is evident, analysing each month independently across years does not clearly distinguish the 2019 eruption from the surrounding years.

To investigate the temporal behaviour in greater detail, the analysis was refined to the monthly level for the year 2019. Figure~\ref{fig:HxC_2019} presents the corresponding feature representations in the four $H \times C$ planes, where each point represents one month of the year. Motivated by this encouraging result obtained from the 10-minute RSAM time series, the subsequent analysis investigates whether the use of the original 1-second RSAM measurements provides an even more detailed characterization of the temporal dynamics preceding and during the eruption.

To further investigate the behavior of the information-theoretic features throughout 2019, the RSAM time series, consisting of 10-minute averaged observations, was analysed using overlapping weekly windows. For each weekly segment, ordinal pattern features were extracted using an embedding dimension of $D=5$, and the corresponding entropy-complexity coordinates were computed. Each weekly observation is represented by a point in the $H \times C$ plane. For clarity, only the observations corresponding to the extreme regions of the feature space are labelled with their associated week numbers, facilitating the identification of the weeks exhibiting the lowest entropy and highest complexity in the Shannon, R\'enyi, and Tsallis planes, and the highest entropy and highest complexity in the Fisher information plane. This representation enables the progression of the weekly dynamics to be tracked throughout the year and facilitates the identification of anomalous behavior associated with the December 2019 volcanic eruption.

Table~\ref{tab:weekly_extremes} summarizes the four weeks exhibiting the strongest structural signatures according to the Shannon, Tsallis, R\'enyi, and Fisher information measures. All four measures consistently identify the same set of weeks, indicating a high level of agreement in detecting periods of increased structural behavior within the seismic time series. Among these, Week~56 (27 November-3 December 2019) exhibits the lowest entropy and highest statistical complexity for the Shannon, Tsallis, and R\'enyi measures, indicating the most ordered ordinal pattern distribution observed during 2019. Likewise, the Fisher information measure identifies the same week as having the highest Fisher information and complexity, reflecting the greatest degree of local regularity in the seismic signal.

The week containing the Whakaari/White Island eruption (Week~57, 3-9 December 2019) is consistently ranked as the third most structurally significant period by all four measures. Since this week includes the eruption on 9 December 2019, these results indicate that the seismic signal remained highly structured immediately before and during the eruption. This behavior is also consistent with the corresponding seismic time series, which shows elevated and persistent seismic activity during the period leading up to the eruption.

Interestingly, two earlier periods, Week~15 (26 March-1 April 2019) and Week~39 (17-23 August 2019), are also consistently identified by all measures as exhibiting relatively low entropy and high complexity. Their locations on the entropy-complexity plane indicate that the seismic dynamics during these weeks were more structured than the surrounding periods, suggesting that significant changes in the ordinal pattern distribution were not unique to the eruption period. These intervals may therefore represent episodes of increased volcanic unrest or changes in the underlying seismic behavior that warrant further investigation.
